Pixels Needed for a 24×36 Print

At a strict 300 PPI a 24×36 inch print would need 7200 × 10800 pixels (77.8 megapixels) — more than most cameras produce. In practice, posters are viewed from several feet away, so 150–200 PPI usually looks sharp.

The maths is print inches × PPI for each side: 24 in × 300 = 7200 px and 36 in × 300 = 10800 px, and 7200 × 10800 = 77,760,000 pixels = 77.76 megapixels (rounded to 77.8 MP above).

Why 150 PPI can be enough at 24×36

24×36 is a 2:3 aspect ratio — the same shape as a standard 3:2 camera sensor, so most camera photos fill the sheet with no crop. The key variable is viewing distance. The eye can only resolve a limited amount of detail, and that limit falls as you step back:

  • A hand-held 8×10 is viewed from about 12 inches, so it rewards 300 PPI.
  • A 24×36 poster is normally viewed from 3–6 feet or more. At that distance, 150 PPI detail is at or beyond what most people can distinguish, and 200 PPI leaves comfortable headroom.
  • Reserve 300 PPI at this size for prints people will inspect nose-close, such as detailed maps, technical drawings or gallery work viewed up close.

Example: a common 6000 × 4000 px photo (24 MP) gives about 167 PPI at this size — 6000 ÷ 36 = 167 on the long side and 4000 ÷ 24 = 167 on the short side — above the 150 PPI poster threshold, so it is a realistic poster candidate, not a failure for missing 300 PPI.
